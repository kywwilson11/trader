# Chapter 9. The life of a trading day

## 9.1 The idea

A trading system is not a prediction. It is a loop. Every half minute it wakes up, looks at the
world, decides whether anything must be done, does it, writes down what it did and why, and goes
back to sleep. Everything interesting about the system (the model, the gates, the risk limits)
lives inside that loop, and every weakness of the system shows up there too: a step that runs in
the wrong order, a quote that arrives late, a decision that nobody recorded.

This chapter follows one day of that loop. It is the most concrete chapter in the book, and it is
worth reading slowly, because every later chapter (training, measurement, the LLM, the hardware)
is ultimately about making this loop do the right thing or about checking whether it did.

Three terms first.

- A **book** is one family of positions managed by one loop. This system has two: the **crypto
  book** (six coins, trades 24 hours a day, 7 days a week) and the **stock book** (a universe of
  US stocks, trades only while the exchange is open). Since the morning of 2026-09-27 only the
  stock book runs; section 9.5 explains why.
- A **cycle** is one pass through the loop.
- A **journal** is the append-only diary the loop writes: one line of JSON for every decision,
  including the decisions *not* to trade.

## 9.2 Why it matters financially

Money is made or lost at specific moments, and the loop decides which moments it participates in.
Four financial facts shape the day.

1. **Costs are not constant through the day.** Spreads (the gap between the best price a buyer
   offers and the best price a seller asks) are widest in the first minutes after the open, when
   overnight news is still being digested. A strategy that trades at 9:31 pays more than the same
   strategy at 10:15. Gao, Han, Li and Zhou (2018) also found that intraday predictability in the
   US market concentrates in the first and last half hours; the middle of the day is mostly noise
   that still charges you a spread.
2. **The night is different from the day.** Lou, Polk and Skouras (2019) showed that a large share
   of the stock market's long-run return is earned overnight, between the close and the next open,
   while prices can also gap overnight past any stop you set. Holding overnight is both where
   return lives and where unprotected risk lives.
3. **Scheduled news moves markets in predictable windows.** Federal Reserve (FOMC) statements and
   CPI inflation prints produce some of the largest moves of the year, and a model trained on
   hourly bars has no special insight into them. Lucca and Moench (2015) documented the unusual
   behaviour of stocks around FOMC announcements.
4. **An exit that is late is an exit that failed.** A stop-loss that is checked after a slow
   housekeeping task, or not checked because a price quote is stale, is protection on paper only.

The loop's structure is an answer to these four facts.

## 9.3 How this system does it

### The process tree

On the Jetson, a systemd user service (`~/.config/systemd/user/trader.service`, generated by
[`scripts/setup_jetson_system.sh`](../../scripts/setup_jetson_system.sh) `--user`) starts
[`run_pipeline.py`](../../run_pipeline.py) with `--combined-bots --bot-only`. A local override file
adds `--stock-only`. The pipeline launches [`run_bots.py`](../../run_bots.py), which runs each book's
loop as a thread in one process (this saves roughly half a gigabyte of memory compared with one
process per book; Chapter 13 explains why that matters). Each loop is an instance of
`BaseTradingLoop` in [`base_loop.py`](../../base_loop.py), specialised by
[`crypto_loop.py`](../../crypto_loop.py) or [`stock_loop.py`](../../stock_loop.py). This is the
"template method" pattern: the base class fixes the order of the steps, and each book fills in
the details that differ (market hours, how a buy order is placed, how the day ends).

### Starting up

`BaseTradingLoop.run` does four things before its first cycle:

1. Starts the optional order stream (only if `TRADER_ORDER_STREAM=1`).
2. Loads the model with `_load_models`. If any model file is missing, the model is `None`, and
   every buy decision later **fails closed**: no prediction means no entry.
3. Cancels stale open orders, but only for this book's own symbols, so one book never strips the
   other book's protective stops.
4. Rebuilds its positions with `_reconstruct_positions` from `stock_position_state.json` (or the
   crypto equivalent): high-water marks, trailing-stop state, cooldowns, the daily trade budget
   and the peak equity used by the drawdown ladder.

Then it enters `while True: self._run_one_cycle()`. An exception inside a cycle is logged and
alerted, and the loop sleeps and continues. The design comment explains why: a crash-restart
cancels working orders and wipes in-memory state, which is worse than skipping one beat.

### One cycle, in order

The order below is the order in `BaseTradingLoop._run_one_cycle`. It is deliberate.

| Step | What happens | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Remote flatten request (a file such as `flatten_stock.flag`, or a Telegram command) | `_check_flatten_request` |
| 2 | Is the market open? Crypto: always. Stocks: the Alpaca market clock, which knows holidays and half days; a wall-clock fallback (9:30 to 16:00 ET, weekdays) if the clock call fails | `check_market_hours` |
| 3 | Circuit breaker: if the account is down 5 % on the day (`CIRCUIT_BREAKER_PCT = 0.05`), flatten and halt entries | `_circuit_breaker_check` |
| 4 | End of day (stocks only): in the last 10 minutes before the actual close, flatten, keeping a small overnight sleeve | `flatten_before_close` |
| 5 | **Exits**: hard stop, take-profit, trailing stop | `_manage_stops` |
| 6 | Hot reload if a new model was promoted | `_hot_reload_check` |
| 7 | Every 10th cycle: macro regime, equity, correlations, account risk | `_update_macro_regime` and siblings |
| 8 | Predictions for every symbol, with a 45 second hard deadline | `_get_predictions` |
| 9 | Hourly challenger "shadow" predictions (no trading effect) | `shadow.maybe_log_shadow` |
| 10 | LLM analysis, at most every 600 seconds (`LLM_INTERVAL_SEC = 600`) | `_run_llm_analysis` |
| 11 | Signal sells, then LLM veto sells | `_execute_sells`, `_execute_llm_veto_sells` |
| 12 | **Entries**, only if buys are allowed | `_execute_buys` |
| 13 | One `cycle_latency` journal row with the time spent in each phase | `_journal_cycle_latency` |
| 14 | Sleep | `_sleep` |

Notice that exits (step 5) run before the hot reload and the maintenance block. The code comment
cites the reason: rebuilding the correlation matrix alone took 11 to 28 seconds, and a protective
exit must never wait behind housekeeping.

The sleep in step 14 is `LOOP_INTERVAL = 30` seconds plus a random jitter of up to 5 seconds
either way, never less than 10 seconds. If the GPU is hotter than `THERMAL_THROTTLE_TEMP = 75` °C
(defined in [`trading_utils.py`](../../trading_utils.py)) the interval doubles. So "a 30 second
cycle" really means "do the work, then rest about 30 seconds". A slow cycle stretches the gap.

### The gates in front of every entry

Before the per-symbol checks, the whole book must be allowed to enter (`_entries_allowed`):

- no manual halt (the file `trading_halt.flag`);
- no scheduled macro stand-down: FOMC statement days from 12:00 to 15:30 ET and CPI days from
  06:30 to 09:30 ET (the `_WINDOWS` table in [`macro_calendar.py`](../../macro_calendar.py));
- for stocks, additionally: the book was not already flattened today, and the clock is inside an
  **entry window**.

The entry windows live in [`strategy_config.py`](../../strategy_config.py):
`STOCK_ENTRY_WINDOWS_ET = [('09:45', '11:00'), ('14:30', '15:30')]` with
`ENTRY_WINDOWS_ENABLED = True`. The comment explains 9:45 rather than 9:30: the first fifteen
minutes are dominated by the opening auction unwinding, with wide spreads and quote-driven
adverse selection. Only *entries* are windowed. Exits and stops run all day.

Then each candidate symbol walks a funnel. The full table is in
[`docs/MAP.md`](../MAP.md) section 4e; the shape of it is: cooldown since the last trade in that
name, a 24 hour lockout after a hard stop, a daily trade budget per symbol, a position cap, a
prediction and a fresh quote must both exist (fail closed), the **cost gate** (the prediction must
beat the round-trip cost including the quoted spread), the model's certified threshold, a
"winner's curse" check for names already stretched far above their average, correlation with
existing positions, VIX limits, the SPY 200-day trend filter for stocks, the sentiment
multiplier, the LLM veto, the meta-label veto, a lower-tail (q10) veto, and finally sizing. The
stock book only considers its top `TOP_N = 7` names by prediction rank, and keeps holding a name
while it stays within `HOLD_RANK = 15`. That gap between the entry rank and the hold rank is a
deliberate "buy/hold spread": Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) found it to be the most effective way
to stop a strategy from paying costs to churn names that hover around a cutoff.

### Sizing

Position size is risk-based (`_compute_position_size`): the amount you are willing to lose if the
stop is hit, `RISK_PCT_PER_TRADE = 0.005` (half a percent of equity), divided by the distance to
the stop. That base is then capped by the per-symbol notional limit and multiplied by a clamped
product of "tilts" (Kelly, volatility target, VIX, drawdown, sentiment, LLM, meta-label and
others), and finally limited by a book-wide risk budget, `MAX_BOOK_RISK_PCT = 0.025`.

### The end of the stock day

`StockLoop.flatten_before_close` runs in the last 10 minutes before the actual close according to
the Alpaca clock (so a half day before a holiday closes early and the flatten moves with it; the
wall-clock fallback is 15:50 ET). It sells everything except the **overnight sleeve**, chosen by
`_select_overnight_keepers`:

- `OVERNIGHT_SLEEVE_ENABLED = True`, at most `OVERNIGHT_SLEEVE_MAX_POSITIONS = 2` names;
- each kept position at most `OVERNIGHT_SLEEVE_MAX_PCT_EQUITY = 0.05` of equity;
- the model must still predict at least `OVERNIGHT_SLEEVE_MIN_PRED = 0.0`;
- no leveraged ETFs, no name with an earnings report ahead, and if the earnings calendar is
  unavailable the sleeve keeps **nothing** (fail closed, because a stop cannot protect against an
  earnings gap);
- no "bounced loser" (a stock that fell during the day and jumped into the close, a pattern that
  tends to reverse by the next open);
- the survivors are ranked 70 % by model prediction and 30 % by overnight momentum, and keepers
  get a good-till-cancelled (GTC) stop before the day orders expire.

It also sells **orphans**: broker positions in the universe that the loop lost track of, so that
nothing unmanaged rides overnight.

The crypto book never closes; its protection overnight is a resting GTC stop-limit order at the
broker, with the limit 2 % below the trigger (`RESTING_STOP_LIMIT_GAP = 0.02` in `crypto_loop.py`).

### What the journals record

[`trade_journal.py`](../../trade_journal.py) writes one file per day, `journals/YYYY-MM-DD.jsonl`.
Its docstring is the contract for every row type: `buy` (prediction, sentiment, LLM score and
multiplier, final notional, decision and fill prices, slippage, the full sizing decomposition),
`sell` (exit reason, P&L), `skip` (which gate said no, plus the prediction it overruled),
`llm_analysis` (the scores the LLM gave and what they cost), `llm_backoff` and `llm_error`,
`entry_window`, `account_risk`, and `cycle_latency`. Rotation is off by default
(`TRADER_JOURNAL_ROTATE_DAYS=0`). The reason every *skip* is recorded is the subject of Chapter 11:
a veto is a counterfactual trade, and you can only price a gate if you know what it blocked.

## 9.4 A worked example

**A stock day, clock in hand.** Take an ordinary Tuesday with no FOMC or CPI release.

- 09:30 ET. The Alpaca clock reports the market open. The loop runs full cycles: exits are
  managed, predictions are computed, but `_in_entry_window` returns False, so no buys.
- 09:45. The first entry window opens. Suppose the model ranks a stock third, its prediction clears
  the cost gate and the certified threshold, and every other gate passes.
- Sizing: equity is $100,000, so the risk budget is $100,000 x 0.005 = $500. The stop sits at
  2 x ATR (the stock policy's `atr_stop_mult`); if the hourly ATR is 1.2 % of price, the stop
  distance is 2.4 %, and $500 / 0.024 = $20,833 of stock. But the stock book's per-symbol cap is
  $5,000 (`MAX_NOTIONAL_PER_SYMBOL` in `stock_loop.py`), so the cap binds and the real risk is
  $5,000 x 2.4 % = $120, about 0.12 % of equity. The lesson: in this system the caps, not the
  elegant formula, usually decide the size. Any claim about "risk per trade" should be checked
  against the cap.
- 11:00 to 14:30. No new entries. Stops, trailing stops and signal exits keep running.
- 14:30 to 15:30. The second window.
- About 15:50 (ten minutes before the close). The flatten runs. Up to two positions that meet the
  sleeve rules are kept with GTC stops; everything else is sold.

**A real cycle.** The first cycle of the crypto loop on 2026-09-27 wrote this `cycle_latency` row:
exits 6.1 s, maintenance 1.9 s, predictions 0.3 s, LLM 30.9 s, total 39.2 s. The LLM call hit its
30 second latency ceiling. That cycle therefore took 39 seconds of work *plus* the 30 second
sleep, so the next cycle began more than a minute later. Nothing broke, but it shows why the
exits run first: had they run after the LLM, a stop would have been checked 30 seconds late.

**A journal that tells a story.** The journal for 2026-04-20 contains 1,110 rows, and every one
of them is a `skip` whose reason reads like `llm_below_buy_min (0.58<0.60)`. In April the code had
a rule that required the LLM score to be at least 0.60 before buying; that rule has since been
removed. Those journals describe a *different policy* from today's, which is why the measurement
tools in Chapter 11 exclude them.

## 9.5 What the evidence says

Be candid about what is known.

- **The design choices are literature-backed, not locally proven.** The entry windows, the
  overnight sleeve and the macro stand-downs each cite a published result (Gao et al. 2018; Lou,
  Polk and Skouras 2019; Lucca and Moench 2015). None of them has been measured on this system's
  own trades, because the current code has not traded with a model. The bots did not run from
  2026-05-07 until the overnight campaign of 2026-09-26/27, and in that campaign no model passed
  certification (Chapter 14), so every buy failed closed.
- **The loop behaved as designed when it did run.** From 03:09 on 2026-09-27 the bots ran in an
  exits-only mode against six inherited crypto positions. The ENGINE department had written a
  fake-broker harness and predicted what the loop would do; the live journal matched every
  observable prediction (six reconstruct warnings, six GTC stops placed, no spurious exits).
- **Stale quotes silently skip exit checks.** A crypto quote older than 180 seconds is treated as
  missing, and a missing quote means no software exit check for that name in that cycle. A
  census found ETH quotes stale in about 15 % of cycles over ten minutes and SOL quotes older than
  180 s in 18 of 24 polls on a quiet Sunday night. The candidate fix (`CRYPTO_QUOTE_MAX_AGE_SEC`,
  default off) waits on an owner decision
  ([ENGINE owner items](../../research/campaign_2026-09_jetson/session_artifacts/generals/engine/OWNER_ITEMS.md), item B.1).
- **The book is currently stock-only.** On 2026-09-27 the founder directed the effort toward
  stocks; the service now runs `--stock-only` and the paper account was liquidated to cash. The
  crypto code is intact and can be switched back on.
